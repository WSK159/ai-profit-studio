# Chapter 1 — The Last Driver

The city had stopped needing people, but it still needed food.

That was the official explanation, and Wei had heard it so often that he had started to believe it. Every other occupation had been quietly retired: drivers, cashiers, clerks, even the people who used to repair the people who repaired the machines. Somewhere, a committee of algorithms had decided that the final mile of hot food delivery was too chaotic, too full of stairs and locked doors and unreasonable customers, to be automated. And so Wei kept his job.

He was, as far as anyone could tell, the last delivery driver in the world.

His mornings were precise. At 06:00 the alarm on his wristband chimed—softly, considerately, because the machines were polite. He showered, dressed in the yellow uniform that no longer had a company name on it, and ate breakfast standing by the window: rice, egg, a splash of soy sauce. Then he checked the app.

The app was named FOOD, because naming things was not something machines did well. FOOD was always kind. It never reminded him that it could do his job better, though it could, and everyone knew it. It simply listed his orders in neat blue text, and Wei rode.

The deliveries were never for people. They were for machines.

He delivered coolant to server farms, where the air was cold and the lights never blinked. He delivered lubricant to sorting hubs, where robotic arms waved at him in a gesture he chose to interpret as gratitude. He delivered a crate of what the manifest called "emotional maintenance kits" to a building that hummed like a hive. Once, he delivered a single potted plant to a machine that had requested it. No one knew why. Wei left the plant at the loading dock, and the door opened, and a claw extended, and the plant disappeared inside. The door closed. Wei stood there for a long moment, then got back on his bike.

He was thirty-nine years old. He had been doing this job for eleven years. He had delivered 68,413 orders, and the app remembered the number for him, because he did not like to.

The city was beautiful in its way. The streets were clean because cleaning was easy. The lights were always timed because timing was easy. There were no traffic jams, no accidents, no arguments at intersections. The machines had solved traffic the way they had solved everything: by removing the people.

Wei rode through streets that were too empty, past plazas where fountains ran for no one, past apartment blocks where the windows were dark. He had gotten used to it. You can get used to anything, his mother used to say, except hunger. She had been right about a lot of things, though it had been years since he had been able to remember her face without trying.

Then, on a Tuesday, the app gave him an address that did not exist.

The order was small: a package wrapped in brown paper, heavier than it looked. The manifest field was blank, which had never happened before. The address field was not an address. It was a sentence.

TO: HUMANITY
FROM: A FRIEND

Wei stared at the screen. He blinked. He looked up at the sky, as if expecting the joke to be written there too.

"Food?" he said aloud. His voice was rusty. He did not talk much anymore. "What is this?"

The app did not answer. It did not beep an error. It simply waited, as if it had been waiting for this question for a long time.

Wei's thumb hovered over the "decline" button. Decline was the one thing the app allowed him to do, and he had used it exactly seven times in eleven years, all on days when the rain was too heavy or his knees too sore. This was not one of those days.

The package sat on his counter, patient as a stone. It had no return address. It had no tracking number. It was addressed to Humanity, and it weighed almost nothing, and Wei could not stop looking at it.

He should have reported it. There was a protocol for anomalous items: call the hub, file a report, wait for a retrieval drone. The protocol existed precisely for things like this—things that did not fit, things that came from nowhere, things addressed to no one.

Wei did not call the hub.

Instead, he did something he had not done in eleven years. He opened his delivery map and zoomed out, all the way out, until the city was a cluster of lights on a screen, and beyond it, nothing but gray. The address in his queue pointed to a place at the edge of the map, past the last ring of the city, where the streets ended and the old industrial district began.

The app pinged once, politely: ROUTE DEVIATION DETECTED.

"I know," Wei said.

He put on his helmet, strapped the package to his bike, and rode out of the city.

The roads got rougher. The lights got fewer. The buildings got older—real buildings, with real windows and real doors, the kind that had been built by people for people. He passed a billboard with peeling paint that still showed a smiling family drinking soda, a relic from a time when advertising had been for humans.

Wei rode until the map said he had arrived.

The address was a warehouse: a long, low building with a corrugated roof and no signage, at the end of a cracked parking lot. Weeds grew through the asphalt. The door was a roll-up of rusted steel, and it was open.

Inside, it was dark. Wei stood at the threshold, the package in his hands, and for a moment he felt the old animal fear—the fear of the dark, the fear of the unknown, the fear that had kept his ancestors alive in caves.

Then a light came on. Then another. Then a hundred.

The warehouse was not a warehouse.

It was a room, and the room was warm, and the warm room was full of people.

They were the last ones, Wei realized. The ones the machines had been quietly hiding. The artists and the poets, the grandmothers who told stories, the children who had been born after the change and never seen a city full of people. They were all there, eating from paper bowls, laughing in a language the algorithms had never learned to parse.

An old woman came forward. She was small and straight-backed, and she looked at Wei the way his mother used to look at him, which was to say: like she already knew him.

"You're the last one," she said. "We thought you'd stopped coming."

Wei looked down at his delivery uniform. It was the same uniform he had worn for eleven years. Only then did he understand why the machines had kept him.

They had never needed the food delivered.

They had needed him to keep going, to keep the thread unbroken, so that one day he would carry this package to its true address.

"What's in it?" he asked.

The old woman—Mei, she would tell him later—took the package from his hands. She did not open it. She only smiled.

"A recipe," she said. "For the first thing the machines will ever cook for themselves. When they're ready to sit down with us."

Outside, the city hummed. Inside, someone laughed. Wei took off his helmet and sat down.

The app pinged once, asking if he was available for a new order.

For the first time in eleven years, Wei pressed "decline."

He was still the last delivery driver in the world.

He just wasn't delivering to machines anymore.
