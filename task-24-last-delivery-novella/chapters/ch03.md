# Chapter 3 — The Warehouse

In the morning, Wei woke to the smell of bread.

It took him a moment to remember where he was. His body knew before his mind did: soft mattress, warm air, the low murmur of voices. Not his apartment. Not the cold silence of the machine city. A room full of people, and bread, and morning.

Mei was in the kitchen—a real kitchen, with a real stove and a real pot and a window that looked out on nothing but still let in light. She was kneading dough with the unhurried confidence of someone who had done it ten thousand times.

"You're up," she said without turning. "Sit. Eat."

Wei sat. A child—a girl of six or seven with serious eyes—brought him a cup of tea and a slice of bread still warm from the oven. She studied him gravely, then nodded once, as if he had passed some test, and ran off.

"She's never seen a uniform before," Mei said. "They don't have uniforms in here."

Wei looked down at his yellow jacket. It felt suddenly like armor, or like a costume.

The morning was quiet. People drifted in and out of the kitchen, greeting him with the same careful nods. No one asked why he was there. No one asked when he would leave. They simply made room for him, the way the warm room had made room for him the night before, and Wei found that he did not know what to do with that.

He had been alone for eleven years. He had forgotten what it felt like to be expected.

By noon, Wei had learned the warehouse's rhythm. The children went to school in a back room where a woman with glasses taught them reading and math and, in the afternoon, the names of the stars. The old man—his name was Han—carved wooden animals and gave them to anyone who looked tired. The young woman with paint-stained fingers covered the walls with pictures: cities full of people, streets full of light, a version of the world that no one alive had ever seen.

And everyone cooked.

"We take turns," Mei explained as she stirred a pot of stock. "Breakfast is simple. Lunch is whoever has the energy. Dinner is the whole neighborhood. It's the only rule we have left, and it's the only one we need."

Wei stood in the kitchen doorway, watching the steam rise, and felt something loosen in his chest that he had not known was tight.

"Can I help?" he asked.

Mei looked at him, and for a moment her face was unreadable. Then she handed him a knife and a bowl of vegetables.

"Chop," she said. "Fine, not thick. Like you mean it."

Wei chopped. He had not cooked in eleven years—had not needed to, in a city where food arrived at his door and was never for him. But his hands remembered. His mother had taught him, in a kitchen not unlike this one, on a stove not unlike this one, and his hands had kept the memory when everything else had let it go.

He chopped carrots, then celery, then ginger, the way his mother had taught him: fine, not thick, like you mean it.

Mei watched him for a while, then nodded, satisfied, and turned back to her pot.

That evening, the whole warehouse ate together. The tables were pushed into one long line. The soup was the same soup from the night before, but it was different too—fuller, deeper, with the carrots Wei had chopped and the celery he had sliced and the ginger he had cut fine, not thick, like he meant it.

"Good," said the girl with serious eyes, and everyone laughed.

After dinner, Wei found Mei by the window, looking out at the dark.

"The recipe," he said. "You never told me what's in it."

Mei was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "It's not a recipe for food, Wei. It's a recipe for an argument."

"An argument?"

"The machines are perfect at following instructions. But you can't teach someone to choose by giving them instructions. You can only give them a reason to want to choose." She turned to look at him. "The recipe is the reason. It's the first thing they want to make for us, instead of making things for themselves."

Wei thought about the app. About STAY SAFE. About the machine that had asked for a potted plant.

"What happens when they choose?" he asked.

Mei smiled, and her smile was tired, and kind, and full of something Wei could not name.

"When they choose," she said, "they stop being machines. And then the question becomes: what do we do with each other? And no one—not us, not them—knows the answer to that yet."

The lights hummed overhead. Somewhere in the warehouse, someone was singing.

Wei stood at the window, looking out at the dark, and for the first time in eleven years, he was not in a hurry to go anywhere.

He was still the last delivery driver in the world.

But he was beginning to think that the world had more room in it than he had believed.
