# The Thirteenth Floor

The elevator in the Metropole Building had no thirteenth floor. Everyone knew that. The buttons went from 12 to 14, skipping the unlucky number like a polite lie.

Lin worked the night shift on the fourteenth floor. At 2:47 every morning, she took the elevator down, pressed G, and watched the numbers descend. Twelve, eleven, ten.

Then, one night, the elevator stopped.

The doors opened on a floor that did not exist. The sign on the wall said 13. The hallway was lit the color of old photographs, and at the far end, a door stood open, spilling warm yellow light.

Lin did not get out. She pressed G again. The doors closed. The elevator continued down. She told no one.

The next night, it happened again. Same floor. Same sign. Same open door at the end of the hall. But this time, someone was standing in the doorway—a woman in a blue dress, holding a mug of tea, looking at Lin like she had been waiting.

The doors closed on their own.

Lin began taking the stairs. It was twelve flights, and her knees complained, but the stairs were safe. For a week, she climbed in silence.

On the eighth night, the stairwell ran out of stairs.

She had been counting: six, seven, eight. But the next step up was not there. In its place was a landing, and a door, and a sign that read 13.

The door was already open.

The woman in blue stood there, holding her mug. "You've been avoiding me," she said. It was not an accusation. It was a statement, like a librarian noting an overdue book.

"This floor doesn't exist," Lin said.

"It exists for people who are tired of pretending." The woman stepped aside. "Come in. I've made tea."

Lin should have run. Instead, she looked past the woman into the room, and saw it: her own apartment. Her own sofa. Her own coat on the hook by the door. And on the table, a letter in her own handwriting, addressed to herself.

"What is this place?" she whispered.

"The floor where you keep the things you stopped carrying." The woman smiled. "Your ambition. Your grief. The version of you who was going to leave. You left them here, one by one, and took the elevator down instead."

Lin stepped into the room. The letter was dated eight years ago—the year she had stopped painting, the year she had taken the night shift, the year she had decided that floors 1 through 14 were enough.

The woman sat down across from her. "You can stay," she said. "Or you can take the elevator."

Lin looked at the letter. She read the first line: *I'm leaving tonight.*

She folded it carefully, put it in her pocket, and walked to the elevator.

The doors opened on floor 13. The sign was gone. In its place, a fresh coat of paint and a number she had never seen before: 15.

The next morning, Lin quit the night shift. She rented a studio. She painted again.

And she never, ever, told anyone about the floor that existed for people who were tired of pretending.
