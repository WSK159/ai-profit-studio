# The Seventh Exhibit

The museum had a room no visitor was allowed to enter. It was small, unmarked, at the end of the Contemporary History wing. The staff called it the Vault. The curator, Dr. Aoki, called it the place where things waited.

She was the only one with the key.

Inside the Vault were six glass cases. Each held a single object: a wooden comb, an old copper coin, a length of red string, a faded photograph, a black umbrella, a cassette tape. No labels. No provenance. Just the objects, arranged in a circle, facing inward, like six people waiting for a seventh to sit down.

Dr. Aoki visited the Vault every evening at closing time. She dusted the glass. She checked the temperature. She never touched the objects, because she knew—had known for thirty years—that the objects touched back.

The comb had belonged to a woman who disappeared in 1976. The coin, to a pawnbroker who vanished from a locked shop. The string, to a matchmaker who walked into the river and left no ripple. Dr. Aoki had catalogued them all, one by one, as the cases filled over the decades.

The seventh case had been empty since the day she started.

Tonight, it was not empty.

Inside the seventh case was a small object, freshly placed, still holding the warmth of a hand. A badge. A museum badge.

Hers.

Dr. Aoki's hand went to her chest. The badge was gone from her lapel.

The six cases were no longer facing inward. They were facing her.

She should have run. Instead, she took a step closer, because the photograph in the fourth case had changed. It was the same photograph—six people at a ferry dock, faces scratched out by a blade—but one of the scratched faces was no longer scratched. It had been replaced, clumsily, by a new face.

Her face.

From the cassette tape, a voice began to play. It was her own voice, younger, recorded before she had ever seen the Vault:

*"If you're hearing this, you've found the seventh. Don't count the others. Count yourself. One, two, three, four, five, six—"*

The voice stopped at six.

Dr. Aoki looked at the empty space in the circle, between the umbrella and the tape. There was a gap just wide enough for a person.

She had always thought she was the curator.

She had been wrong. She was the exhibit—the last one, the one the museum had been collecting toward for thirty years. The comb, the coin, the string, the photograph, the umbrella, the tape: they had not been waiting to be displayed.

They had been waiting for her to complete the set.

Dr. Aoki picked up her badge. Her fingers were steady. She pinned it back on her lapel, opened the door of the seventh case, and stepped inside.

The door closed behind her.

In the morning, the museum opened as usual. The Vault was locked. The cases were all full. And in the guidebook, printed fresh that day, there was a new exhibit listed at the end of the Contemporary History wing:

No. 7—The Curator Who Understood.

Visitors were not allowed to enter. But they always stopped to look at the door, and the door, very faintly, looked back.
