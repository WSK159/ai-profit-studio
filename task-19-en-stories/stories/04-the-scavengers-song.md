# The Scavenger's Song

The derelict ship was singing.

Juno heard it on the long-range receiver first—a thin, warbling melody drifting across eleven million kilometers of dead space. Her instruments labeled it "anomalous emission, unclassified." Her gut labeled it something else: trouble with a price tag.

She was a scavenger. Trouble was the inventory.

The ship had no name on its hull, no registry in the archives. It was the size of a city block, and it had been dead for a very long time—the kind of dead that left the lights on. Juno docked her little skiff, cut through the airlock, and stepped into a corridor that was clean, warm, and singing.

The song came from everywhere. It was not a recording. It was the ship itself, humming through its own bones, as if the walls had learned a lullaby and could not stop.

She found the bridge. The chairs were empty. The coffee cups were still warm.

On the captain's console, a single line of text glowed:

*They are not lost. They are being sung.*

Juno had heard stories about the Singing Ships—derelicts that drifted into inhabited space, broadcasting music until someone answered. The official explanation was a malfunction. The unofficial explanation, whispered in salvage bars, was that the ships were carrying passengers who had been "filed" somewhere the universe kept its quiet things.

She should have stripped the reactor and left.

Instead, she sat in the captain's chair, because the song had changed. It was not a lullaby anymore. It was a question, shaped like a voice she almost recognized.

Her mother's voice.

Her mother had been on the colony ship Meridian, lost forty years ago. Juno had stopped hoping, then stopped remembering, then stopped talking about it. But the song knew. The song had her mother's pitch, her mother's particular way of ending a sentence like a question.

"Mom?" Juno said.

The song stopped.

For one full second, the ship was silent—the absolute silence of forty years of waiting. Then every speaker in the vessel spoke at once, in a chorus of thousands:

*We are not lost. We are being sung. Will you sing us back?*

Juno looked at the console. A single button had appeared, labeled in no language she knew but which she understood perfectly:

ANSWER.

She put her hand on it. The ship held its breath.

"I don't know the words," she said.

And the ship—her mother's voice, gentle, from somewhere very far away—answered:

*Then hum. It's enough to begin.*

Juno hummed. The first note was small, off-key, afraid.

The ship joined in. And somewhere in the dark between stars, a colony of forty years of missing people began, one by one, to remember their own names.
